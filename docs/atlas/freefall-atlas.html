Phenomenon A · Droplets

The d² law, and where it stops

FLEX PSI-69FLEX-2 PSI-68CFI PSI-39PSI-117measuredderived fit
A

What the theory predicts

For an isolated, spherically symmetric droplet burning quasi-steadily, the evaporation rate is proportional to the diameter, so the square of the diameter falls linearly: d² = d₀² − K·t. This is the classical result of Godsave and Spalding1,2. On Earth buoyancy breaks the symmetry; on the ISS it does not, which is why FLEX is the cleanest test of the law ever flown4.

What the data adds

Test #107 follows the law closely: a straight-line fit gives a burning-rate constant within 2% of the value NASA reports. The flame does not follow the same line. Its diameter grows from about 9 to 13 mm while the droplet shrinks, so the flame standoff ratio rises from about 3 to about 5, whereas quasi-steady theory predicts a constant ratio. Hickman and co-authors report that at low oxygen the burning rate itself departs substantially from quasi-steady predictions22.

Why students should see this

A linear d² is necessary for quasi-steady burning but not sufficient. Reading the droplet and the flame together is the first habit of a combustion experimentalist.

Fills a production TODO. The test page reserves a box for “our own linear fit of K from these points, labelled as an estimate next to NASA’s K”. This fit, with its window and residual, is that box.

Points to watch in the data

  • The last d² point of #107 is 0.0 and the flame diameter jumps to 34 mm. Both coincide with the disruption of the droplet and are best treated as tracking artefacts.
  • The first ~50 frames include deployment, igniter heating and needle retraction. Fit K after them.
  • FLEX-013 has a 1.3 s gap (40 missing frames at 30 fps). Joining it with a line invents data and biases K.
  • ZeroedDropletX/Ypos is a processing origin: on translated-fiber tests such as FLEX-043 it sits about 18 mm from the droplet.

Key points from the papers

  • FLEX has run 276 single-droplet tests in 225 unique conditions. Burning rates, flame standoff ratios and extinction diameters agree reasonably with simplified theory and with detailed simulation for methanol22.
  • At low oxygen the burning-rate constant deviates substantially from quasi-steady predictions: only transient burning occurs22.
  • A linear d² for #107 is a property of the droplet. The flame has its own history (FSR panel).

Where it goes

  • Test page, section “Droplet: the d² law”: fit, window, residual, trap tags.
  • Test page, “Flame and droplet diameter”: add FSR as a third trace.
  • Phenomenon page /phenomena/d2-law: the explanation, once.

Phenomenon B · Spacecraft fire safety

Radiative extinction: big flames starve themselves

FLEX PSI-69BASS-II PSI-25 (context)measuredcontext
B

Two ways to go out

A droplet flame can go out in two ways. Diffusive extinction happens at high oxygen with small droplets; radiative extinction happens at low oxygen with large droplets8,23. For methanol, FLEX also recorded diffusive extinction linked to water absorption by the droplet8,24. The FLEX Research Requirements Document cites Chao, Law and T’ien (1990) on the effect of flame radiation on extinction9,23 and shows both modes in earlier droplet data: diffusive for the small droplet, radiative for the large one.

What the 274 tests show

Bigger droplets go out bigger. For free heptane droplets, extinction diameter tracks initial diameter with r ≈ 0.84. Tests #70 and #73 (4.6 mm at 15% O₂ in N₂) reported extinction diameters of 4.6 mm, essentially their initial size. The diluent matters too: helium gives clearly higher K (median about 0.60 mm²/s against 0.48 in N₂), CO₂ slightly lower (about 0.46). The CO₂ tests cover a narrower oxygen range, so compare diluents at equal O₂. Nayagam and co-authors treat He and Xe as alternatives to the CO₂ used in the US ISS module30.

Why it matters beyond the lab

NASA-STD-6001 flammability tests are run in normal gravity (Test 1 is upward flame propagation)19. FLEX found that microgravity can sustain flaming combustion at lower oxygen concentrations than normal gravity, and that for quiescent flames radiation, not convection, decides how a flame extinguishes22. BASS-II, listed as coming soon in production, is planned to compare its microgravity limiting oxygen with the normal-gravity worst case defined in NASA-STD-600131.

The lunar-habitat question

Spacesuits operate at pressures down to 4.3 psia (29.6 kPa)18. For habitats with frequent spacewalks, NASA’s Exploration Atmospheres Working Group recommended in 2006 that landers operate at 10.2 psia with 26.5% O₂ and at 8.0 psia with 32% O₂; after a 2012 re-evaluation the second was changed to 8.2 psia with 34% O₂. The Shuttle used 10.2 psia with 26.5% O₂ on 34 missions18. NASA describes these environments as mildly hypoxic: the oxygen partial pressure is about 19 kPa, a little below the 21 kPa at sea level, while the oxygen fraction is much higher. Flammability was one of the three trade-offs the working group weighed, with hypoxia and decompression sickness18.

FLEX has 17 free and fiber tests in the dashed box. None ended in self-extinction: every one finished in disruption or burned to completion. The sample is small and covers two fuels.

Points to watch in the data

  • 77 of 274 tests have no extinction diameter. Disruption and completion leave nothing to measure.
  • Fiber-supported tests lose heat to the fiber; never pool them with free droplets.
  • FLEX reports a limiting oxygen index for methanol (about 12–13% O₂ at 1 atm) and n-heptane8,22. The lowest O₂ values in the table are test conditions, not the LOI.

Key points from the papers

  • Below roughly 14% O₂, radiative extinction occurs rather than extinction from water absorption, and burning is transient22.
  • FLEX determined the limiting oxygen index for methanol and n-heptane; for methanol it is about 12–13% O₂ at 1 atm8,22.
  • Microgravity sustains flames at lower O₂ than normal gravity. For a quiescent flame, radiation rather than convection decides how it goes out22.
  • Cool flames can extend the flammability of fuels in microgravity30.

Where it goes

  • Overview: this scatter replaces a static count; each dot opens its test.
  • Overview preset “How does it burn in a lunar atmosphere?”: add the box and the 17/0 result.
  • Compare “How they differ”: the diffusive vs radiative explanation when d₀ differs.

Phenomenon C · Droplets · chemical kinetics

Cool flames: burning after the flame goes out

FLEX PSI-69CFI PSI-39PSI-117PSI-142published modelmeasured quote
C
Stable cool flameOscillatory cool flame
“The droplet continues to vaporize linearly after visible flame extinction and there is a large vapor cloud formed nearly coincident with a plateau…”researcher comment, FLEX test #050 · TestSheet COMMENTS

The discovery

In 2012 Nayagam and colleagues reported that large heptane droplets on the ISS kept shrinking at a steady rate after their visible flame had radiatively extinguished5. Something was still burning, invisible to the cameras. It was a cool flame: a soot-free, low-temperature reaction22 (the PSI-117 simulations stay between about 600 and 700 K). Farouk and Dryer reproduced the two-stage behaviour in detailed simulations6.

Why the chemistry allows it

Alkane fuels add O₂ to the fuel radical and isomerise through RO₂ and QOOH species. Because the formation of RO₂ and QOOH is thermodynamically reversible, the overall reaction rate stops rising with temperature: this is negative temperature coefficient (NTC) behaviour7,27. FLEX reports second-stage cool-flame burning first for n-heptane and then for n-octane, n-decane and n-dodecane8; large droplets burn quasi-steadily in this regime after radiative extinction, while smaller droplets burn to completion or extinguish disruptively32.

From droplets to other studies

CFI flew the follow-up with n-dodecane and other fuels. PSI-117 shows in simulation that an external thermo-convective perturbation can turn a stable cool flame into an oscillating one. PSI-142 holds counterflow-flame data: its table lists cool-flame extinction limits measured with and without ozone16. Cool flames can extend the flammability of fuels in microgravity, which is why they matter for fire safety30. One phenomenon page can carry all four studies.

Points to watch in the data

  • A cool flame is soot-free and low-temperature22, so the visible-light record ends while vaporisation continues.
  • Radiometer clocks start at their log, not at camera t = 0; align them manually before reading a transition.
  • end_type describes how the test ended as recorded; the comment of test #050 shows vaporisation continuing after visible extinction.

Key points from the papers

  • Second-stage, low-temperature vaporisation after the visible flame was not expected from the experiment design or from quasi-steady theory22; in over 50 years of microgravity droplet experiments it had not been seen7.
  • The sequence in large n-heptane droplets: radiative extinction, second-stage cool-flame burning, cool-flame extinction, vapour cloud8.
  • The same behaviour is seen in n-octane, n-decane and n-dodecane8.

Where it goes

  • Overview preset “What are cool flames?”: the explanation and the #050 quote.
  • New studies: PSI-117 and PSI-142 as cards under Combustion Science › Droplets.
  • Test page: a “possible cool flame” chip on heptane tests whose d² keeps falling after visible extinction.

Phenomenon D · Measurement science

Soot shells and the limits of a backlit camera

D

Derived aggregate from Rasul et al., Tables 1 and 311. Propylbenzene alone accounts for 7 of the 11 occlusions.

The physics

On Earth soot rides the buoyant plume upward and leaves. In microgravity two forces hold it near the droplet. The Stefan flow of evaporating fuel pushes particles outward, weakening as 1/r². Thermophoresis pushes them down the temperature gradient, which inside the flame points back toward the cool droplet. Where the two balance, particles stall, collide and agglomerate into a spherical soot shell10.

The measurement problem

HiBMs images the droplet in silhouette against a backlight. The shell projects onto the droplet edge, and an edge detector finds the edge of an aggregate instead of the liquid surface. Rasul and co-workers quantified this for FLEX-2: clean frames are measured directly to a fraction of a pixel, granular soot forces a grid search with larger error, and full occlusion leaves only a prediction from earlier frames11. Earlier automated pipelines met the same limits12,13.

Why interns should know it

PSI diameter files do not carry a per-frame uncertainty. A sooty interval looks exactly as authoritative as a clean one. Anyone fitting K or validating a model on heptane or aromatic blends needs to know which frames to trust.

Points to watch in the data

  • No quality flag in DropletData: soot-affected frames are indistinguishable from clean ones.
  • Diameter noise grows during luminous, sooty phases (TestSheets: “luminous flame obscures droplet”).
  • The workbench’s blue/orange/red quality colours are a model, never a NASA flag.

Key points from the papers

  • The soot shell sits between droplet and flame, where the drag from the outward Stefan flow equals the thermophoretic force25.
  • After radiative extinction no further soot forms, but the shell radius expands. A possible reason is weaker thermophoresis once the hot flame is gone28.
  • The image analysis assumes the soot shell, flame and droplet are concentric, so the silhouette is a body of revolution12.

Where it goes

  • Library: a “measurement” topic grouping Rasul, Dembia, Yu & Shaw.
  • Test page: a soot-risk tag on heptane tests whose comment mentions sooting or obscuration.
  • Learning mode: the workbench’s “measure it yourself” game, labelled as synthetic.

Phenomenon E · Droplets · fuels

Disruption: how often, and what it does not mean

FLEX PSI-69FLEX-2 PSI-68measuredtext-mined
E

What the record says

61 of 274 FLEX tests ended in disruption: the droplet deformed, shattered or tore off its fiber, usually close to extinction. Heptane disrupted more often than methanol in nitrogen (18 of 47 against 12 of 54). Production defines the label carefully: disruption means self-extinction was not observed, not that the flame would have burned forever.

What it does not mean

“Microexplosion” is the classical mechanism studied for multicomponent droplets26. FLEX fuels are single-component, so that mechanism needs care. For methanol, FLEX shows the droplet absorbing water during burning, which links diffusive extinction to the liquid becoming a methanol–water mixture8,24,21; a researcher comment on test #112 mentions “maybe a small gas bubble popping”. In the n-alkane experiments, large droplets burn into the cool-flame regime while smaller droplets burn to completion or extinguish disruptively32. The TestSheets describe disruption without assigning a mechanism. FLEX-2, with its decane, heptane, iso-octane, toluene and propylbenzene blends, is where microexplosion belongs.

Points to watch in the data

  • Counting the word “disrupt” gives 89 tests; only 66 comments affirm it.
  • dext_mm is often the diameter at the moment of disruption, not at extinction.
  • The final frames before disruption contain zeros and spikes (see test #107).
  • Sources can disagree: test #112’s comment reports “a little disruption”, while its end_type is Completion.

Key points from the papers

  • For n-alkanes, large droplets continue burning in the cool-flame regime after radiative extinction; smaller droplets burn to completion or extinguish disruptively32.
  • For methanol, diffusive extinction is linked to water absorption by the droplet8,24.
  • FLEX-2 extends the study to bi-component and surrogate fuels22.

Where it goes

  • Test page “What happened”: the end type plus the affirming sentence from the comment, highlighted.
  • Learning mode: the “what happened at the end?” quiz, answered from end_type.
  • FLEX-2 study, when listed: the multicomponent chapter.

Phenomenon F · Fluid physics

Flow, drift and body forces

FLEX PSI-69PSI-117ACME E-FIELD (context)context
G

Microgravity is not zero flow. Igniter impulses and bumps against the needles give free droplets a drift of millimetres per second; many TestSheets end with the droplet leaving the HiBMS field of view. Fiber tests are deliberately translated at 3 mm/s. Each of these is a weak forced convection that breaks the spherical symmetry the d² law assumes. PSI-117 simulates the effect of an external thermo-convective perturbation on cool-flame dynamics14.

ACME’s E-FIELD Flames investigation is a different kind of microgravity experiment: it applies electric fields to methane coflow flames aboard the ISS. A 2022 study reports the first mapping of how the ion-driven wind produced by the field changes flame behaviour in the absence of buoyancy29. The ion wind is a body force on the charged parts of the flame, which can be studied without buoyancy mixing it in. That study does not present itself as a partial-gravity simulation.

Key points from the sources

  • “The droplet drifts SE after deployment, hits the igniter, changes direction and quickly moves W out of HiBMS FOV.” test #010 comment
  • E-FIELD maps the effect of an ion-driven wind on flames in microgravity, without the confounding influence of buoyancy29.

Where it goes · Test page: a trajectory panel once the API serves X/Y. ACME: a study card under Combustion Science when data are added.
